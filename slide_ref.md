📊 Academic Justification for User Metrics in Behavioral Finance Game Design
In the field of behavioral finance education, interactive simulations that reflect real-world decision-making contexts have been shown to enhance both engagement and learning outcomes (Benartzi & Thaler, 2007; Fernandes et al., 2014). To faithfully simulate financial decision environments and expose players to common behavioral biases, our game integrates five core user metrics—each grounded in psychological and economic theory.

1. Age
Theoretical Justification: Age acts as a temporal anchor for framing financial decisions across the life-cycle. In behavioral economics, life-cycle theories (Modigliani & Brumberg, 1954) highlight how consumption and savings behavior vary with age and expectations of future income.

Behavioral Insight: People often exhibit time-inconsistent preferences, such as overvaluing present consumption early in life (Laibson, 1997). Representing age explicitly helps contextualize this bias.

2. Net Worth
Theoretical Justification: Net worth serves as an objective representation of accumulated financial outcomes from a sequence of decisions. It supports modeling of intertemporal choice and wealth effects.

Behavioral Insight: Individuals often exhibit the house money effect—a tendency to take more risks when they perceive prior gains as “free money” (Thaler & Johnson, 1990). Tracking net worth makes this bias salient in later stages.

3. Annual Income
Theoretical Justification: Income is a core component in rational financial planning models (Friedman, 1957), and acts as a proxy for utility and lifestyle sustainability.

Behavioral Insight: Income perception can anchor decisions through projection bias—players might overestimate the stability or growth of current income streams (Loewenstein, O’Donoghue, & Rabin, 2003).

4. Emotional State
Theoretical Justification: Emotions are increasingly recognized as a vital component of financial decision-making (Loewenstein et al., 2001). Emotional tracking aligns with affect heuristic models, where emotional reactions substitute for analytical judgment.

Behavioral Insight: Negative affect is associated with more conservative decisions, while positive mood can increase risk tolerance (Isen & Patrick, 1983). Including emotion allows for state-dependent utility modeling.

5. Investment Portfolio
Theoretical Justification: Portfolio tracking simulates repeated investment contexts, key to understanding heuristics and biases in probabilistic environments (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979).

Behavioral Insight: Players may show status quo bias, mental accounting, or loss aversion when adjusting portfolios, especially under simulated market fluctuations.

🎓 Educational Purpose of Metric Visibility
Making these metrics visible and responsive in real-time reinforces experiential learning (Kolb, 1984). Players can see how each decision affects their financial trajectory and emotional state, creating a feedback-rich environment that promotes:

Reflection on long-term vs. short-term trade-offs

Recognition of personal cognitive and emotional patterns

Deeper understanding of theoretical constructs through action
